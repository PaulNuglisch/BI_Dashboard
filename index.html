🔍 Business Problem

A multi-regional retail company wants to understand the key drivers of revenue and profitability across its five operating regions (North, South, East, West, Central). Despite growing marketing spend across five channels — including Paid Ads, Social Media, Email, Marketplace, and Organic Search — revenue growth has been inconsistent month-over-month and across product categories. Management needs a data-driven view of which segments, channels, and product categories generate the most value, and whether advertising investment and website traffic are effectively converting into revenue. A linear regression model is applied to quantify these relationships and support forward-looking revenue forecasting.

Regression Interpretation
What the OLS model suggests about each variable's relationship with Revenue_USD
⚠️
Always verify these interpretations against your Google Colab output. The coefficients below come from an OLS regression fit on all 365 orders with no train/test split. Check the model.summary() (statsmodels) or coef_ / intercept_ (scikit-learn) values in your notebook to confirm signs, magnitudes, and p-values. A low R² (0.164) means most revenue variance is unexplained — treat every interpretation as tentative and correlational, not causal.
Units Sold
▲ Appears to increase revenue
Coefficient +12.15 — the model suggests that each additional unit sold is associated with approximately $12.15 more in expected revenue, holding ad spend, website visits, and discount rate constant. This is the largest positive predictor and aligns with intuition: selling more units directly scales revenue. The effect may also partly reflect that higher-priced product categories tend to sell in higher quantities. 🔎 Verify: check that your Colab output shows a positive coefficient for Units_Sold and that the p-value indicates statistical significance.
Discount Rate
▲ Appears to increase revenue (use caution)
Coefficient +154.85 — the model suggests that a 1 percentage-point increase in discount rate (e.g., 8% → 9%, encoded as 0.08 → 0.09) is associated with roughly $1.55 more in expected revenue. The positive sign is counterintuitive, since discounts reduce unit price. A likely explanation is that higher discounts are applied to larger or higher-priced orders, creating a misleading positive association. 🔎 Verify: confirm the sign and magnitude of the Discount_Rate coefficient in Colab. A positive value here does not mean discounting is a revenue-growth strategy.
Website Visits
▲ Appears to increase revenue
Coefficient +0.43 — the model suggests that each additional website visit is associated with approximately $0.43 more in expected revenue. The positive direction is consistent with the expectation that higher traffic leads to more purchasing activity. However, the per-visit effect is modest, and the relationship may partly reflect shared seasonality — both visits and revenue may rise together during peak periods rather than one causing the other. 🔎 Verify: check the Website_Visits coefficient sign in Colab. If positive, the direction is consistent with this interpretation, though the magnitude may differ depending on how the model was specified.
Ad Spend (USD)
▼ Appears to decrease revenue (likely confounded)
Coefficient −0.94 — the model suggests that each additional dollar of ad spend is associated with roughly $0.94 less in expected revenue. This is almost certainly not causal. A plausible explanation is reverse causality or confounding: ad spend may be concentrated on lower-priced product categories, or campaigns may be intensified precisely during weak-demand periods. This result should not be interpreted as evidence that advertising reduces revenue. 🔎 Verify: confirm the negative sign of Ad_Spend_USD in your Colab output. Consider whether the model includes sufficient controls (e.g., product category, price) to isolate the ad spend effect.
Customer Rating
— Not included in this regression model
Customer_Rating was not used as a predictor in this OLS model, so no coefficient or directional effect can be reported here. Descriptively, the dataset shows a mean rating of 4.39 / 5.0, with little visible spread at the order level. Whether higher ratings are systematically associated with higher revenue cannot be determined from this model alone. 🔎 Verify: if your Colab notebook includes Customer_Rating as a predictor, note its coefficient sign. If it is not included, consider adding it to a new model version and comparing the R² to assess whether it improves explanatory power.
Model Fit & Overall Limitations
R² = 0.164  |  n = 365  |  OLS, no split
The model explains only 16.4% of total revenue variance, meaning 83.6% is driven by factors not captured by these four predictors. This is typical in retail settings where product mix, pricing tiers, promotions, and customer behavior all interact. All coefficient interpretations above are conditional associations — not causal estimates — and are sensitive to omitted variables, multicollinearity, and the absence of a validation set. 🔎 Verify: cross-check the R², F-statistic, and individual p-values in your Colab model.summary() table before drawing conclusions or presenting results.
